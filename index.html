Three questions I came away with from the radioligand therapy work, and where I think I could contribute.

A

three questions, after reading closely · tap one

from A single-arm, low-dose, prospective study of 177Lu-EB-PSMA radioligand therapy in patients with metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer  ·  Journal of Nuclear Medicine 64(4), 2023  ·  and project 10.32 on the NUS Medicine list.

The description for 10.32 is explicit that both the radionuclide and the targeting ligand can be chosen per patient, by tumour size and tumour type. That is a genuinely personalised premise, and personalisation of a radiation dose eventually has to produce a number in grays, to the lesion and to the organs at risk.

Getting that number is quantitative imaging. Serial post-therapy SPECT or PET, activity quantified in each volume of interest at each time point, a time-activity curve fitted through them, integrated to a cumulated activity, then converted to absorbed dose.

Most centres administer a fixed activity instead, not because dosimetry is uninformative but because it is laborious: multiple return visits, manual segmentation on every time point, curve fitting per lesion. The laborious part is the part I would automate.

this is a pipeline problem wearing a clinical hat.

The JNM study is described in its own title as single-arm and low-dose, which is the right and cautious way to begin with a new agent. It establishes safety and it shows the ligand reaches its target.

The question it opens is the relationship between administered activity, absorbed dose at the lesion, and response. Administered activity is the thing that gets prescribed. Absorbed dose is the thing that does the work, and the two are related by the patient’s own biodistribution and clearance, which vary a great deal between people.

A dose-response curve is what turns a protocol into a prescription, and it is reconstructable from imaging that a trial is often already acquiring for other reasons.

the data for this is usually already on the PACS.

The registered FAPI PET/MRI protocol in hepatocellular carcinoma, NCT05687747, images fibroblast activation protein. FAP-targeting radioligand therapy delivers radiation to that same target. That pairing is the whole theranostic premise: the scan is supposed to tell you who will benefit and roughly how much.

That premise is quantitatively testable rather than merely plausible. Does pre-therapy PET uptake in a lesion predict the post-therapy absorbed dose in that same lesion? If it does, the diagnostic scan becomes a dosimetry predictor and patient selection gets a real basis. If it does not, the pairing needs rethinking before it scales.

This is the question on the whole NUS Medicine project list I would most like to work on, partly because it spans two projects rather than sitting inside one.

it connects 7.11 and 10.32, and nobody owns the join.

why this group

Four years of getting trustworthy numbers out of noisy signals, and a year now on the manufacturing side of the machines that produce them. A dog’s bark. A child’s gaze. A radio wave off a moving body. Then MRI, PET and hybrid PET/MRI at NIMHANS, and Siemens Healthineers.

Radioligand therapy is the one place in imaging where the number you compute is the dose a patient receives. I would rather work somewhere the measurement has consequences than somewhere it only has a p-value.

Simulation. A mono-exponential clearance with a patient-specific effective half-life, sampled with measurement noise at the selected time points. The single-time-point arm assumes a population-average half-life rather than fitting one, which is how it is usually done in practice. Illustrative only, not a dosimetry model for any agent.

D

what the reading buys

With one time point you are not measuring this patient’s clearance, you are substituting a population average for it. That is defensible as a screening estimate and indefensible as the basis for a tailored prescription.

The error has a direction that matters clinically. A patient who clears faster than average gets their tumour dose overestimated, and a slow clearer gets their kidney dose underestimated. The second one is the patient you would most want to catch.

Press the resample button a few times on two points and the honest wrinkle shows up: counting time points is not the same as placing them well. Two closely spaced noisy measurements fit a slope over a short lever arm, and can occasionally do worse than one point plus a sensible prior. The fix is not simply more scans, it is scans at times chosen to constrain the decay.

Going from one time point to three or four is not a research programme, it is a scheduling decision plus an analysis pipeline. The scheduling is the hard part clinically and the pipeline is the part that keeps it from being worth doing.

So my pitch is narrow and concrete: build the pipeline so that serial dosimetry costs an afternoon of compute instead of a week of someone’s segmentation time, and the dose-response question stops being theoretical.
